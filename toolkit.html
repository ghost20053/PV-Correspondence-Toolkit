Developer Toolkit

Six design considerations, a reflective worksheet and a worked calibration example for examining physical–virtual interaction.

Six Design Considerations

These questions reproduce the terminology and order of Table 1 in the exegesis. They are practice-derived prompts for reflection, not sequential steps or a validated assessment framework. See how the four findings informed them.

01

Intended Action

What physical contact does the action promise?

Define the action and the contact or feedback the player should expect.

Prototype example: Resting hands on the table requires a reliable contact surface; grasping a cube requires compatible hand positioning.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

02

Physical Counterpart

Which available object can support that action?

Choose a real object for its relevant contact surfaces, size and stability.

Prototype example: The tabletop supplies a stable surface and spatial reference; cubes and a chest supply attainable physical contact surfaces.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

03

Correspondence

Which properties must match for the action to make sense?

Identify the dimensions, positions, orientations or movements that matter for this action.

Prototype example: The tabletop needs to correspond in height and location. Moving a cube also raises orientation and motion-matching demands.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

04

System Knowledge

What can the hardware actually detect?

Separate sensed information from assumptions about physical objects and system state.

Prototype example: Tracked hands supply live position data; the engine cannot independently detect untracked prop orientation, and occlusion can affect grasping.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

05

Starting Arrangement

What physical layout does the design assume?

Make the initial physical conditions explicit and consider what happens if they change.

Prototype example: The marked cloth establishes repeatable hand and prop positions without tracking subsequently moved objects.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

06

Onboarding

How will the player discover and establish that arrangement?

Help the wearer locate the physical setup and perform a meaningful calibration action.

Prototype example: Passthrough reveals the real table; hands-on-table calibration replaces reaching for virtual targets.

Related research finding · Toolkit resource

RESOURCE 02 — COMPLETED EXAMPLE

Case Study: Hands-on-Table Calibration

A documented revision from virtual fake-hand targets to physical tabletop contact in the original Honours prototype. Read this alongside Finding 5.2, journal Sections 15–16 and 21, and calibration screenshots and recordings.

PROJECT Tangible Interaction Honours Prototype
HARDWARE Meta Quest 3
INTERACTION Physical–Virtual Tabletop Alignment
TRACKING Tracked Hands / Untracked Props
DESIGN PROBLEM

Technical detection does not guarantee meaningful physical correspondence.

The initial calibration approach used virtual fake-hand targets. Although this provided a detectable hand-position condition, the targets were not anchored to the physical tabletop.

The procedure was revised to use direct physical contact with the tabletop as the calibration action.

Development Process

Expand each stage to examine the design decisions documented during development.

01 PLAN — Intended Interaction ⌄

Before the player begins grasping or placing virtual representations of real objects, the virtual scene must relate meaningfully to the physical tabletop.

Calibration should establish a useful spatial reference between the tracked hands and the real surface.

02 TEST — Initial Approach and Revision ⌄

The initial approach asked the wearer to align their hands with virtual fake-hand targets, providing a gesture condition the application could detect.

During development, this was revised to placing both hands directly on the real tabletop during setup.

This documents a design change, not a controlled comparison of two methods.

03 OBSERVE — Development Findings ⌄

The fake-hand targets could satisfy the technical detection condition, but their placement was not anchored to the real tabletop.

This could encourage a hand position inconsistent with the physical contact required later in the experience.

No quantified accuracy comparison or unfamiliar-participant results were recorded.

04 REFLECT — Design Interpretation ⌄

The problem was not simply whether hand tracking recognised a gesture.

The calibration action needed to establish a meaningful relationship between the wearer's body, the physical table and the virtual representation.

Direct tabletop contact provided a tangible reference that connected intended action, spatial correspondence and onboarding.

05 REVISE — Design Response ⌄

The resulting approach used hands-on-table calibration rather than abstract virtual hand targets.

A controlled tabletop layout provided repeatable starting positions for untracked physical props.

Passthrough instructions helped the wearer locate the real objects before the virtual scene was revealed.

Further testing would be required to establish suitability for unfamiliar users.

What Developers Can Learn

  • Define the intended physical action first.
  • Check that technical success corresponds with meaningful real-world contact.
  • Distinguish tracked information from assumptions about untracked objects.
  • Use repeatable starting arrangements when continuous prop tracking is unavailable.
  • Treat onboarding and calibration as part of interaction design.

Evidence and Limitations

This example is based on one developer's iterative practice in the original Honours prototype. It is not an independent participant study or controlled experiment.

The example does not establish improved accuracy, comfort, immersion or usability for other players.
