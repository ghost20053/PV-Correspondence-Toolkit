HONOURS RESEARCH APPENDIX

Physical–Virtual Correspondence

This project explores how physical objects and their virtual representations can support meaningful interaction in virtual reality. Its central concern is the relationship between what a player sees, what they touch and what the system can actually track.

This overview summarises the project, its development insights and the materials collected here. Follow the links for the demonstration, evidence, prototype and practical toolkit.

THE PROJECT

Connecting what we see with what we touch

The original Honours prototype pairs a virtual tabletop scene with real objects, including a table, cubes and a chest. A Meta Quest 3 tracks the wearer's hands. The physical props are untracked, so the experience relies on a controlled arrangement and repeatable starting positions.

Passthrough instructions help the wearer find the real objects before the virtual scene is revealed. Hands-on-table calibration provides a physical reference for aligning the experience. The accompanying demonstration shows this original prototype in use.

DEVELOPMENT INSIGHT

A detected gesture is only part of the interaction

An early calibration approach asked the wearer to match virtual hand targets. The application could detect the hand-position condition, but those targets were not anchored to the real tabletop. Technical detection alone therefore did not establish the physical relationship needed for later interaction.

The procedure was revised to placing both hands on the real table. This brought calibration, physical contact and onboarding into the same action. The wider design lesson is to examine whether a system's success condition corresponds with what the player is physically doing.

RESEARCH RECORD

An account of iterative development

The supporting materials record design decisions, experiments and revisions through a reflective journal, 468 screenshots and 85 video entries. The archive retains original filenames so observations can be traced back to their development evidence.

The work is grounded in one developer's iterative practice. It is not an independent participant study or a controlled comparison, and does not establish improved accuracy, comfort, immersion or usability for other players.

PRACTICAL CONTRIBUTION

Six considerations for physical–virtual design

The toolkit turns the development reflections into six questions: what action is intended; which physical object supports it; what must align; what the system knows; how the arrangement starts; and how the player is introduced to it.

A reusable worksheet follows Plan, Test, Observe, Reflect and Revise. It supports documenting one interaction or design revision, separating observations from interpretations and identifying what remains untested. A completed calibration example shows how that process can be used.

EXPERIENCE & SCOPE

Trying the original prototype

The Windows build is the original Honours artefact. It runs on a PC and requires the relevant VR and physical tabletop setup; it is not a standalone Quest installation. The setup guide documents the arrangement and identifies measurements and software configuration that still need verification.

Later QR-code tracking experiments are a separate extension and are outside this appendix's original replication procedure. The toolkit is a reflective design aid rather than a validated usability assessment. The exegesis itself is not currently available to download here.
